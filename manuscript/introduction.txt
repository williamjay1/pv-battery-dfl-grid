1. Introduction

Residential batteries are increasingly operated by controllers that already optimize an announced electricity tariff. In this setting, a forecasting improvement has value only through the actions it changes. Replacing a statistical forecasting loss with realized operating cost can produce such a change, but the availability of a differentiable controller does not establish that the additional learning system is worth maintaining. A small price benefit may be recoverable with a few calibration parameters, a scenario controller may handle uncertainty more effectively, and the action preferred by a household may create a less favorable voltage pattern elsewhere on its feeder. The engineering question is therefore whether decision-focused training changes method selection once these alternatives and consequences are considered together.

Decision-focused learning connects prediction to a downstream optimization objective [L12,L13]. Differentiable optimization layers make this connection practical for constrained decisions [L14,L15], and a broad review documents both its promise and its dependence on the optimization problem and learning formulation [L16]. Energy applications include wind scheduling [L05], storage arbitrage [L06], PV–battery operation [L04], robust energy management [L02] and joint renewable learning in distribution networks [L03]. These studies motivate evaluating decision value separately from forecast error. They do not imply that a cost-trained predictor is the preferred engineering implementation in every residential control setting.

The closest residential study is Depoortere et al.'s decision-focused PV–battery scheduling model [L01]. It already examines the mismatch between forecast error and operating value, pretrained initialization and dependence on load-prediction quality. Those observations are established motivation rather than contributions of the present article. Alkhulaifi et al. also show that decision-focused storage models need not dominate all alternatives [L28]. The contribution sought here is more specific: an evaluation of the incremental private and feeder consequences of changing the training objective, followed by tests of whether that increment survives simpler representations, different controller boundaries and deployment inputs.

Feeder consequences require a separate assessment. Forecast-uncertain prosumer scheduling [L07], tariff and grid-charge design [L08] and studies of tariff-driven storage operation [L27] show why household settlement and network operation cannot be represented by one scalar objective. Price-responsive devices can also concentrate demand in the same inexpensive intervals [L09]. Network-aware scheduling explicitly incorporating carbon or other system objectives addresses a different problem [L33]. In our study, the household learner receives neither feeder voltages nor network constraints. Any voltage change is a consequence of its altered injections, not an optimized voltage guarantee. Moreover, a fixed-tariff operational replay does not describe the price feedback that could accompany widespread deployment [L10].

We investigate this question using measured Ausgrid consumption and PV traces, simulated residential batteries, and public AC feeder models. Two comparisons are kept distinct. The first controls model initialization, update budget, data and downstream checkpoint selection to estimate the effect of replacing continued squared-error training with a decision loss. The second compares deployable implementations, including training-only low-dimensional calibration, direct net-load prediction and residual-scenario control. Computational cost, household-level benefits and harms, voltage severity and system scale are evaluated alongside average operating cost. A frozen-model transfer to a separate public residential dataset tests portability of the control signal; it does not validate the constructed Ausgrid feeder as an observed network.

The resulting contribution is a conditional method-selection account. It connects the signal change to battery action, nodal injection and AC voltage; tests the source-voltage and controller conditions under which the effect matters; and identifies where additional learning complexity provides little private value or transfers poorly. This is an application and validation study, with mature learning and optimization components. Its value rests on the distinctions it establishes between objective attribution, practical alternatives and engineering consequences, rather than on a new neural architecture or a universal superiority claim.
